A Digital Text Analysis

Love, Loss, and Language

A comparison of Edgar Allan Poe's poetry and William Shakespeare's sonnets using Voyant and Antconc Tools.

Introduction

This comparison uses Voyant Tools to examine the poetry of Edgar Allan Poe and William Shakespeare's sonnets. Although both writers explore human emotions and relationships, their writing approaches these subjects differently. Poe frequently explores grief, death, loneliness, and psychological suffering, while Shakespeare's sonnets examine love, beauty, time, relationships, and mortality.

Edgar Allan Poe

Grief, Darkness, and Mortality

The Voyant analysis of Poe's poetry contains 46,816 total words, 6,922 unique word forms, a vocabulary density of 0.148, and an average of 21.7 words per sentence. These figures suggest a substantial collection with a considerable amount of repeated vocabulary.

The word cloud highlights words such as "night," "bells," "soul," "heaven," "heart," and "long." These words connect to several recurring subjects in Poe's poetry, including death, spirituality, emotional suffering, and isolation. The prominence of "night" reflects the dark and mysterious settings frequently associated with his writing. Words such as "soul" and "heaven" suggest an interest in spiritual questions and what may happen beyond death. The word "heart" emphasizes the emotional experiences of his speakers, particularly grief, love, and loss.

The frequency graph also provides insight into how Poe distributes language throughout his poetry. Words such as "night," "love," "like," "heart," and "bells" appear at different frequencies across the displayed sections. Their changing frequency suggests that Poe does not rely on the same vocabulary equally throughout his work. Instead, particular words become more prominent depending on the poem and its subject.

The writing syle supports the idea that Poe's poetry frequently creates an atmosphere of mystery, sadness, and emotional intensity. His repeated use of language connected to darkness, spirituality, and the human heart helps establish a recognizable literary style.

William Shakespeare

Love, Beauty, Time, and Human Relationships

The Voyant analysis of Shakespeare's sonnets contains 42,870 total words, 6,432 unique word forms, a vocabulary density of 0.150, and an average of 21.1 words per sentence. This vocabulary density is slightly higher than Poe's, suggesting a marginally greater proportion of unique word forms within the Shakespeare corpus.

The word cloud emphasizes words such as "heaven," "night," "heart," "soul," "beauty," "spirit," "eyes," and "time." These words connect to some of the most recognizable subjects throughout Shakespeare's sonnets. Love and beauty are particularly important, as many sonnets explore admiration, romantic relationships, and the desire to preserve beauty. The repeated appearance of words associated with time also reflects Shakespeare's interest in aging, change, and mortality.

The frequency graph displays selected words including "sweet," "shall," "self," "love," and "doth." Their frequencies change throughout the collection, demonstrating that Shakespeare's vocabulary shifts between sonnets. The word "love" is particularly relevant because relationships and affection are recurring subjects throughout the collection. However, Shakespeare does not present love as a single, unchanging experience. His sonnets also explore jealousy, distance, disappointment, beauty, and the effects of time.

His writing style suggests that Shakespeare uses emotional and descriptive language to explore the complexity of human relationships. His vocabulary connects personal experiences with larger questions about beauty, aging, and the passage of time.

Similarities and Differences

Poe and Shakespeare share several linguistic patterns, particularly their use of words associated with the heart, soul, night, and human emotion. These similarities suggest that both writers were interested in exploring personal experiences and the emotional challenges of being human.

However, their overall literary approaches differ. Poe's poetry places greater emphasis on grief, darkness, death, and spiritual uncertainty. Shakespeare's sonnets more frequently explore love, beauty, relationships, and the passage of time. Although both writers discuss mortality and emotional suffering, they approach these subjects through different moods and literary perspectives.

Feature Edgar Allan Poe William Shakespeare
Prominent words Night, bells, soul, heaven, heart Beauty, time, eyes, heart, spirit
Main themes Grief, darkness, death, and spirituality Love, beauty, relationships, and time
Overall mood Dark, lonely, and unsettling Reflective and focused on affection and beauty

The corpus statistics also reveal some similarities. Poe's collection contains 46,816 total words, while Shakespeare's contains 42,870. Poe's corpus therefore contains 3,946 more total words. However, Shakespeare's vocabulary density is slightly higher at 0.150 compared with Poe's 0.148. This suggests that Shakespeare's collection has a marginally higher proportion of unique word forms, despite having fewer total words.

Both collections have similar average sentence lengths, with Poe averaging 21.7 words per sentence and Shakespeare averaging 21.1. This small difference suggests that the two corpora have relatively similar average sentence lengths, although it does not necessarily mean that their poetic structures or writing styles are identical.

Voyant Conclusion

The Voyant Tools analysis reveals meaningful patterns in the poetry of Edgar Allan Poe and William Shakespeare. Both writers use repeated language associated with human emotion, but their vocabulary helps distinguish their literary interests. Poe's poetry frequently connects to grief, darkness, spirituality, and mortality, while Shakespeare's sonnets emphasize love, beauty, relationships, and the passage of time.

The corpus statistics provide additional evidence for comparing their writing. Poe has a larger total word count and more unique word forms, while Shakespeare has a slightly higher vocabulary density. Their average sentence lengths are also relatively close. These differences show how text analysis can provide another perspective on literary works beyond traditional close reading.

Ultimately, Voyant Tools helps reveal patterns across large collections of writing that might otherwise be difficult to recognize. However, word frequency alone cannot explain the full meaning of a poem or sonnet. The most useful analysis comes from combining these numerical patterns with an understanding of each writer's language, themes, and historical context.

The results reveal interesting similarities and differences in the treatment of love and death in the two authors' works.

One particularly interesting finding is the relationship between love and death. In Poe's corpus, love is strongly connected with loss and mortality. His references to Annabel Lee illustrate the idea that romantic love can continue even after the death of the beloved.

In Shakespeare's corpus, love is explored through a wider range of emotional and philosophical situations. Death, meanwhile, is frequently connected with the passage of time and the physical destruction of human life. Nevertheless, the memory of love can remain beyond death.

AntConc Conclusion

The AntConc analysis demonstrates that love and death are significant themes in the literary works of both Edgar Allan Poe and William Shakespeare.

The frequency results show that Shakespeare uses the word love much more frequently than Poe, even when corpus size is taken into consideration. This suggests that love is a particularly prominent theme in the selected Shakespeare corpus.

on the other hand, the normalised frequency of death is slightly higher in Poe's corpus, indicating the importance of mortality in his literary writing.

The KWIC concordance analysis provides further insight into these findings. Poe frequently associates love with idealised romantic relationships, beauty, loss and death. Shakespeare, on the other hand, explores love through a wider variety of emotional experiences and presents death as a philosophical reflection on time, memory and human existence.

Overall, although both authors share an interest in love and death, they develop these universal themes in distinctive ways. The combination of quantitative frequency analysis and qualitative concordance analysis makes it possible to identify not only how often these words occur, but also how they contribute to each author's literary representation of human emotions and mortality.

Key Findings: The strongest finding is not simply that Shakespeare uses love more often, but that the normalised frequency of death is almost identical in both corpora, with Poe showing a slightly higher rate. This provides an interesting basis for comparing their different literary treatments of love and mortality.
